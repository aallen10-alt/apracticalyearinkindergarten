🧰 Teacher Toolbox — Supporting Every Learner ▼
⏱️ Sheep Timer — The Digital Classroom Timer

What it is: A free online visual timer that shows animated jumping sheep counting down. Kids can see exactly how much time is left without asking "how much longer?" It runs on your smartboard, laptop, or projector — no download, no login, completely free.

How to introduce it on Day 1: Pull it up on your screen. Say: "This is our sheep timer. When I start the sheep, that means we are working. When the sheep stop, that means we are done. Watch the sheep — they will tell you when to stop." Start a 2-minute timer. Watch. Done. They will never forget it.

When to use it: Morning work, independent math, writing time, clean up, transitions, centers — any time kids need to know how long they have.

Why it works for every learner: Visual timers reduce anxiety for kids who struggle with time transitions. They remove the constant "how much longer?" question. They create a natural, non-confrontational signal to stop working that no child can argue with.

📚 Math Shelf — What It Is & How to Introduce It

What the math shelf is: A dedicated shelf, bin, or basket in your classroom that holds math manipulatives and early finisher activities. When a student finishes their math work, they go to the math shelf independently — no asking, no waiting, no disrupting. It is the early finisher system for all of math, all year long.

What goes on it: Pattern blocks, linking cubes, dominoes, counting bears, number puzzles, dot cards, ten frames with counters, simple card games. Rotate items monthly so it stays fresh.

How to set it up: Label each bin clearly with a picture AND a word. Every item has a home. Students learn to take and return independently. This takes 2 weeks of practice — it does not happen on Day 1.

How to introduce it — exact script: "After you finish your math page, put your pencil down. Then walk quietly to the math shelf. Pick one thing. Bring it back to your spot. Use it quietly until everyone is done. When I give the signal, everything goes back." Walk 2-3 students through it while everyone watches. Do it again. Then let the whole class try with a practice page.

The rule: You may only take one thing at a time. You return it before you take something else. The shelf stays neat. If the shelf is messy, shelf time is paused until it is neat again.

Why it matters: Early finishers who have nothing to do become behavior problems in under 60 seconds. The math shelf eliminates that. Completely.

🧠 ADHD & Focus Challenges

Before the lesson: Give a preview. "In two minutes we are going to the rug for morning meeting." Transitions are harder without warning. Always warn before switching activities.

On the rug: Seat this student closest to you — not in the back, not on the edge. Proximity is your first and most powerful tool.

During instruction: Give one direction at a time. Wait for compliance before giving the next. Never stack three directions on a child who is still processing the first one.

Movement: Build in micro-movement every 8-10 minutes. A quick stretch, a thumb up/down response, a turn-and-talk. Movement is not a reward — it is a regulation tool and a brain tool.

Fidget tools: A stress ball, a seat cushion, or a resistance band on the chair leg are not distractions — they are regulation supports. Introduce them privately, not as a big announcement.

Visual schedule: Post the daily schedule with pictures. Point to it constantly. Predictability reduces anxiety and increases focus for every child — but especially for kids with ADHD.

Sheep timer: Visual timers are one of the highest-impact tools for kids with attention challenges. Use it for every independent work period.

📋 IEP, 504 & Accommodation Reminders

Know your paperwork: Before school starts, read every IEP and 504 in your class. Highlight the accommodations section. These are legal documents — not suggestions.

Common kindergarten accommodations and what they look like in practice:

• Extended time: Let the student continue while others transition. They do not need to rush. Build natural stopping points into your lessons.

• Preferential seating: Usually means close to the teacher, away from distractions, or near the door for easy bathroom access. This is your call on where exactly — make it intentional.

• Reduced workload: If the page has 10 problems, circle 5. If the writing prompt is 3 sentences, accept 1. Document what you modified.

• Verbal responses accepted: Some students can tell you the answer but cannot write it yet. That counts. Write their response for them and note it.

• Movement breaks: Schedule these proactively — do not wait for dysregulation. A planned 2-minute movement break prevents a 20-minute meltdown.

• Visual supports: Word walls, anchor charts, picture schedules, and manipulatives are accommodations that help everyone but are legally required for students whose IEPs specify them.

When in doubt: Follow the IEP. If you are not sure what an accommodation means in practice, ask your case manager before the year starts — not during a lesson.

🗣️ Speech, Language & Communication Needs

Students with speech services: Know when they are pulled and protect that time. Do not schedule your most important instruction during their pull-out if you can avoid it.

In the classroom: Give extra processing time after asking a question. Count to 10 silently before calling on someone else. Rushing a child with language processing needs teaches them not to try.

Partner talk: Kids with language needs often do better in 1-on-1 conversations than in whole-group. Turn-and-talk is one of your most powerful tools for these students.

Never finish their sentences: Let them find the word. Your patience is the intervention.

Selective mutism: Some students will not speak in group settings — especially in September. Accept non-verbal responses (thumbs up, pointing, nodding) and build trust before pushing verbal participation. Forcing it makes it worse.

✏️ Fine Motor & Sensory Needs

Pencil grip: Correct grip matters but September is not the time for battles about it. Get them writing first. Address grip in October when routines are solid.

Adaptive tools that help: Pencil grips, slant boards, weighted pencils, larger-diameter crayons, triangular crayons. Keep a small supply in your desk.

Sensory seekers: These students need input. Give it to them proactively — a weighted lap pad, a chewy necklace (with parent permission), a fidget, a job that involves carrying something heavy. If they are seeking sensation, they cannot learn until they get it.

Sensory avoiders: These students are overwhelmed by input. Loud rooms, bright lights, crowded spaces, and unexpected touch are painful for them — not dramatic. Reduce sensory load where you can: seat away from the hallway, dim one bank of lights, give advance notice before fire drills.

Fine motor activities that build skills naturally: Playdough, cutting, lacing, tongs, tweezers, threading beads. Build these into center time so they feel like play, not therapy.

🌡️ Behavior & Dysregulation

Big problem vs. small problem: Teach students to ask themselves "Is this a big problem or a small problem?" Practice with examples before you need it in a real moment.

Small problems (solve yourself): crayon broke, someone looked at my paper, I dropped something, I don't have an eraser.

Big problems (get a grown-up): someone is hurt, someone will not stop after I asked them to stop, I feel very sick, there is an emergency.

When dysregulation happens: Lower your voice — do not raise it. Get on their level. Name the emotion without judgment: "I can see you are really frustrated right now." Do not add more words after that. Let it land.

Natural consequences: If a student knocks materials off a desk, they help pick them up. Not as punishment — as a logical outcome. Keep your tone matter-of-fact.

The rug: Students who cannot stay regulated on the rug may need an alternative — a chair at the edge, a floor spot near their cubby. Access to the learning is the goal.

🏥 Anxious Students & Frequent Nurse Visits

First, validate: "Your tummy feels funny. I hear you." Never dismiss a physical complaint — but track the pattern. Most nurse trips spike before transitions or during challenging tasks.

Build the relationship first: An anxious child who trusts you will tell you what is actually wrong. Your relationship is the intervention.

Give them a job: Purpose reduces anxiety. Line leader, paper passer, attendance helper — responsibility is regulating.

Create a safe signal: Work with the student privately on a signal they can give you when they need a moment — a hand on their heart, a specific look. This gives them agency without disrupting the class.

Teach them to name it: "When your body feels worried, where do you feel it?" Naming the feeling reduces its power.

🫁 Nervous System Regulation on the Rug

Why this matters: Five-year-old nervous systems are not fully developed. A calm adult body next to a dysregulated child body is literally how children learn to regulate. You are their external nervous system until they build their own.

Box breathing (30 seconds): "Breathe in for 4 counts, hold for 4, out for 4, hold for 4." Do it with them. Your calm breath is contagious.

Brain shake: Shake hands loose, shake arms, shake shoulders, shake whole body, freeze. Releases physical tension in under 60 seconds. Use it after sitting too long.

Starfish breathing: Hold up one hand, spread fingers wide. Use the other pointer finger to trace up each finger slowly (inhale) and down each finger (exhale). Five fingers, five breaths. Silent and portable.

Mindful Marshmallow Activity 🍬
Aligned to: NJSLS-SEL 2.1.2.A.1 — Self-Management: Students demonstrate self-awareness by identifying and managing emotions and behaviors. Also supports mindfulness, sensory language, and alliteration (ELA connection).

⭐ SPARKLE NOTE: This is a mindfulness teaching tool — never a reward. Always check student allergens and district food policies first. You may substitute a smooth stone, cotton ball, or raisin — the sensory awareness is the lesson, not the food itself.

What to say: "Hold this in your hand. Look at it. What color is it? What shape? Press it gently — what does it feel like? Now bring it close to your nose. What do you smell? Now take the tiniest bite you can possibly take. Chew as slowly as you can. What do you taste? What does it feel like?"

Debrief: "When we slow down and pay attention to one thing at a time, our brain gets quiet. That is what mindfulness feels like. You can do this anytime your brain feels too busy."

Alliteration connection: Say together: "Magnificent, mindful, marshmallow moment." Repeat three times, each time more slowly. Alliteration + mindfulness = two skills, one activity.
